The Tides of Kotka: Dawn in the Brackish Estuary

At four in the morning, the Bay of Bengal exhales. The low diesel rumble of our expedition launch, M.V. Chhuti, cuts abruptly, leaving in its wake a profound, primordial silence. We drift into the Katka canal on silent oars. Dense sheets of river mist weave through the vertical breathing roots—the pneumatophores—of thousands of Heritiera fomes trees, protruding upward like ancient wooden stalagmites waiting for the sea.

The Sundarbans does not submit to cartographic certainty; it is a geography sculpted exclusively by the astronomical pulse of the moon. Every six hours, millions of cubic meters of salty tide surge inland through tens of thousands of serpentine distributaries, erasing all footpaths and leaving only the canopy visible before receding into shimmering mudflats.

Tracking the Ghost: Pugmarks on Saline Clay

Landing at Jamtola Beach requires stepping off the tender boat into knee-deep, suction-grip clay. Our two Forest Department guards, armed with vintage Lee-Enfield .303 rifles, chamber their rounds with an echoing metallic click. Here, the Royal Bengal Tiger (Panthera tigris tigris) is not a museum piece; it is an omnipresent psychic presence. You almost never see the tiger first. It watches you from behind a screen of golpata palms, smelling the salt and sweat on your skin.

Barely forty paces from the water's line, Ranger Kabir freezes. He points his bayonet downward. Fresh, sharp paw prints—measuring 14 centimeters across—are pressed deeply into the wet clay. Water is still slowly pooling into the heel cushion. The tiger crossed this exact clearing less than twenty minutes prior, heading south toward the secluded expanse of Jamtola sea beach.

Encounters with Spotted Deer, Otters & Azure Kingfishers

Further along the meadow behind the Katka watchtower, the tension of tracking predators softens into breathtaking spectacle. A herd of over fifty Chital (spotted deer) grazes lazily alongside troops of Rhesus macaques. The mutualism between these two species is famed throughout Bengal: the monkeys toss fresh tender leaves from high tree branches and deliver shrill warning shrieks the moment a tiger or python stalks into view.

Along the silted banks of Kochikhali, we spot a family of smooth-coated otters hunting river crabs in tight sync, followed by the brilliant electric flash of a Black-capped Kingfisher plummeting into the brackish current. Over three hundred avian species call this delta home, turning the afternoon hours into an auditorium of calls, whistles, and the heavy rhythmic wingbeats of giant hornbills.

Field Guide: Permits, Houseboats & Protocols

Essential guidance compiled with Bangladesh Forest Department officials

wb_sunny

Optimal Season: Nov to Feb

Calm sea conditions, minimal rain, and moderate humidity. Avoid monsoon season (June–Sept) when cyclones and severe tidal surges make navigation dangerous.

security

Armed Guard Mandate

By law, every tourist vessel entering the Wildlife Sanctuary zones must employ two armed Forest Department rangers boarded from Mongla or Chandpai.

receipt_long

Entry Fees & Permits

Foreign tourists: ৳1,500/day + VAT; Domestic travelers: ৳150/day. Professional camera gear incurs a nominal equipment registration surcharge.

eco

Strict Leave-No-Trace

No single-use plastics are permitted off-boat. Drone flights require clearance from the Ministry of Environment and are strictly prohibited in core breeding reserves.
